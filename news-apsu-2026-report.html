ASAP-Bio co-organises APSU 2026, putting animal science education on the agenda in Uganda

2nd APSU Scientific Conference and AGM · Kampala, Uganda · 6–7 November 2026

← Back to News

Conference · Uganda · 6–7 November 2026

Participants of the 2nd Animal Production Society of Uganda Scientific Conference and AGM at the Golden Tulip Canaan Hotel, Kampala.

The 2nd Scientific Conference and Annual General Meeting of the Animal Production Society of Uganda (APSU) took place at the Golden Tulip Canaan Hotel in Kampala on 6 and 7 November 2026. ASAP-Bio co-organised the conference with APSU. It brought together researchers, lecturers, students, policy makers, development organisations and the private sector, with participants from Uganda, Ethiopia, Kenya, Denmark, the Netherlands and Italy.

The theme was “Sustainable Animal Industry Transformation: Innovations in Climate Smart Livestock Production and Service Systems for Vision 2040, and the Human Capital Agenda in Animal Sciences”. The last part of that theme, the human capital agenda, is where ASAP-Bio came in. Together with APSU we gave the education of animal scientists its own place on the programme, next to the science.

Opening the conference

John Kaija, President of APSU, opened the conference, followed by Grum Gebreyesus (Aarhus University) on behalf of ASAP-Bio and Getinet Ashenafi, Academic Vice President of Debre Berhan University. Getinet called for closer cooperation between universities in the region on shared problems such as climate change, feed shortages and livestock diseases that cross borders, and for more student and staff exchange to tackle them together.

Obin Gordon (VSF Belgium) gave an overview of the current state of Uganda’s animal industry. Keynote addresses came from Maholo Dennis Malongo, Assistant Commissioner, on the role of APSU in transforming the Ugandan livestock industry; from John David Kabasa, who asked what the livestock sector needs from university curricula and whether universities are delivering it; and from the representative of Hon. Lt. Col. (Rtd) Bright Rwamirama, Minister of State for Animal Industry.

Education on the main programme

A full session on the first morning was given to animal science education, with contributions from Ethiopia, Kenya and Denmark:

  • Grum Gebreyesus (Aarhus University) presented the ASAP-Bio Knowledge Hub, an open-access, low-bandwidth platform for modernising animal science teaching across East Africa.
  • Mekete Bekele and colleagues (Debre Berhan University) looked at how graduate competencies can keep pace with climate change, digital livestock technologies and the changing needs of the sector.
  • Hiwot Bekele and colleagues (Debre Berhan University) presented a competency-based look at animal science education in Ethiopia, built on the experience of students and early-career professionals.
  • Johnson Kinyua and colleagues discussed the role of academic institutions in mentorship, using the circular economy in animal production as a case.
  • J.G. Maina and colleagues (University of Nairobi) presented teaching and learning approaches that support sustainable agricultural value chains.

ASAP-Bio workshop: what should graduates be able to do?

The afternoon of the first day was an ASAP-Bio workshop on animal science curricula, introduced by Robert Onzima. Short talks first described how animal science is taught today in Uganda (Busuulwa and Sadat Walusimbi), Kenya (Rawlynce Bett and Johnson Kinyua) and Ethiopia (Getinet Ashenafi and Mekete Bekele).

John David Kabasa then chaired a panel on what the sector needs from graduates now and in the future, with voices from the Poultry Association of Uganda, Heifer International, the National Agricultural Research Organisation (NARO), academia and the Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries (MAAIF).

Participants then split into five groups: feed and nutrition, animal health, genetics and genomics, digitisation and big data, and climate resilience. Each group worked through the same four questions: what should animal science curricula aim for, what is missing today, who needs to act to make the change possible, and which emerging trends must be included. Group leads reported back in plenary, and Robert Onzima and David Buule drew the conclusions together. The workshop was facilitated by David Buule, Sandra Langi, John David Kabasa and Mastula Namubiru.

Research across the value chain

Across the two days, presentations and posters covered black soldier fly larvae as feed, mycotoxins in poultry feed, community-based goat breeding in Karamoja, the genetics of indigenous chickens and goats, reproduction and semen handling in cattle and pigs, antimicrobial use on smallholder pig farms, lumpy skin disease in the cattle corridor and climate-smart dairy value chains. On the second day Donald Kugonza chaired a panel on improving animal welfare in Uganda.

The conference closed with the APSU General Assembly, which discussed the society’s strategic plan for 2027 to 2032.

What comes next

APSU 2026 came one week after the ESAP 2026 conference in Addis Ababa. Together, the two conferences gave ASAP-Bio a national conversation in Ethiopia and in Uganda on what animal science graduates need to know and be able to do. These conversations feed into the curriculum work at the partner universities.

We thank APSU for the partnership, and everyone who presented, chaired, facilitated and took part.

ASAP-Bio is an African–Danish university partnership in integrated sustainable animal production systems, funded by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Denmark and managed by Danida Fellowship Centre.
